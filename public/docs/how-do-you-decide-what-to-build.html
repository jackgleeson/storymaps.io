How do you decide what to build?

2 min read

On this page
Where agile starts What fills the gap today Next

Agile is great at helping you build things. Short cycles, working software, and room to change course as you learn. But the two methods most teams mean when they say "agile", Scrum and Kanban, both assume there's already a backlog of work.

Agile needs a backlog to get going. It doesn't say where a good one comes from. That's the gap.

This page looks at what fills it today.

Where agile starts

  • Scrum starts with a Product Owner and a product backlog. The Scrum Guide says the Product Owner sets the Product Goal and orders the backlog, but not how the goal or the first backlog come about. That's deliberate: the guide calls Scrum "purposefully incomplete". Plenty of teams run a "Sprint 0" first, but it isn't in the guide, and it's usually spent choosing the tech stack and setting up, not deciding what to build.
  • Kanban manages the flow of work that already exists. One of its core principles is "start with what you do now", so choosing the work sits outside it.
  • The Agile Manifesto values working software, collaborating with customers and responding to change. It doesn't say how to choose what to build in the first place.

Some agile frameworks do plan up front. XP starts with an Exploration phase, DSDM has Feasibility and Foundations, and Disciplined Agile has Inception. Even these assume someone has already chosen the idea. They refine an idea rather than find one. And they're not what most teams run day to day.

What fills the gap today

It varies a lot from team to team, but it usually goes like this:

  1. 1Ideas come in from everywhere. Customers, support, sales, analytics, leadership and the team itself.
  2. 2The product manager frames and ranks them. Raw requests become problems worth solving, often on a Now, Next, Later roadmap.
  3. 3Discovery tests the big ones. Interviews, prototypes and spikes check an idea holds up before anyone commits to it.
  4. 4Someone writes it up. A product brief, a hypothesis, a requirements document or a pitch: the problem, who has it, what success looks like and what's out of scope.
  5. 5The write-up becomes a backlog. Epics, user stories and acceptance criteria, refined until they're ready for a sprint.

Then Scrum or Kanban takes over.

Busy teams often jump straight from step 1 to step 5. A stakeholder asks for something, the Product Owner writes a ticket, and it goes into the next sprint. Bigger companies lean the other way, with long documents and sign-offs.

Note. Either way, all five steps happen outside the framework, and the delivery team often sees only the result: the backlog.

Next

Most projects fill the gap with a document, then a list of tickets. The problem with big documents and long lists looks at what goes wrong there, and what a story map does instead.
